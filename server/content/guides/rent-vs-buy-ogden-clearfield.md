# Rent or buy?

*Deciding*

*Educational information, not financial or legal advice*

![New construction townhome living, dining and kitchen](images/blog-hero-model-home.webp)

Most people compare rent against principal and interest. That's the wrong comparison, and it's why the answer comes out wrong.

## What does owning really cost each month?

Principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance if applicable, HOA dues if applicable, and maintenance. On a townhome the association handles a good share of the maintenance, which is part of what the dues buy. On a detached house that line is yours and it's lumpy.

Add the cost of getting in and out. Closing costs on the way in, and selling costs on the way out. Those are the numbers that make short holding periods expensive.

## What does renting really cost?

Rent, renter's insurance, and whatever the increase looks like each year. That last one is the piece that gets underweighted. A fixed-rate mortgage payment is fixed on the principal and interest portion for thirty years. Rent isn't fixed for thirty months.

> **Time horizon decides it** — Under about five years, transaction costs usually favor renting. Past that, the fixed payment starts to do the work.

## What do I get for the extra cost of buying?

A payment that stops rising on its largest component. Principal reduction, which is forced saving rather than a return. Appreciation, which is real but unpredictable and should never be the primary reason. And control over the place you live.

## What does renting get me?

Flexibility, which has genuine value if your job or your household might change. No exposure to a roof, a furnace or a special assessment. And liquidity, since your money isn't locked into an asset that takes a couple of months and several percent to convert back to cash.

## Which question should I answer first?

Not "is buying better." It's "am I staying put for long enough, and is the payment comfortable in a bad month." If the answer to both is yes, the math usually follows. If the answer to either is no, the math almost never rescues it.

## How do I run the comparison with real numbers?

Take your actual rent and your actual expected increase. Take the actual price of a specific home, the rate you're actually quoted, real taxes, real insurance and the real HOA. Compare those two columns over the number of years you honestly expect to stay.
