A lender will tell you what you qualify for. That's a different number from what you should spend, and the gap is where people get into trouble.

## How do lenders calculate what I can afford?

Lenders look at your debt-to-income ratio: the total of your monthly debt payments, including the new housing payment, divided by your gross monthly income. The new housing payment means principal, interest, taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues, not just principal and interest.

Different loan programs allow different ratios, and automated underwriting can approve higher ratios when the rest of the file is strong. Credit score, reserves and down payment all move the ceiling.

## Why does my approval feel higher than my budget?

The ratio is calculated on gross income. You spend net. Between federal and state tax, Social Security, Medicare, health insurance and retirement contributions, a meaningful share of that gross number never reaches your account.

A payment that produces an acceptable debt-to-income ratio on paper can still consume an uncomfortable share of what actually shows up on payday.

## What does the debt-to-income ratio miss?

Underwriting counts debts that report to credit. It doesn't count childcare, groceries, fuel, phone bills, insurance premiums you pay annually, or the fact that you're saving for something. Those are real and they come out of the same account as the mortgage.

> **Run the number backwards** — Start from what you're willing to spend each month, then work back to a price. It's a more useful direction than starting from the approval letter.

## What’s a better way to set my budget?

Decide what monthly housing payment you would be content with in a bad month, not an average one. Enter that figure into a calculator with a realistic rate, realistic taxes and the actual HOA, and solve for the price. That's your working budget.

Then get preapproved so you know the ceiling exists, and shop below it. The approval is a boundary, not a target.

## How much cushion should I leave for the first year?

New construction takes furniture, window coverings, a lawn if you've one, and a hundred small things. Buyers who spend to the top of their approval usually spend the first year uncomfortable. Keeping some of the seller-paid incentive as cash at closing rather than putting all of it into the rate is one way to build that room in.
