# The lower payment with a catch

A temporary buydown gives you a smaller payment for the first year or two of the loan. The money that makes that possible has to come from somewhere, and where it comes from is the whole question.

## How it works

Someone pays a lump sum up front, and that money sits in an account subsidizing your payment. In the common versions the subsidy runs one, two or occasionally three years, shrinking each year until you're making the full payment. Your loan never changes. The rate on your note, the amount you borrowed and the payment you'll make in year three were all set on day one, and you qualify at the full payment, not the reduced one.

That's the honest frame for the whole decision: a temporary buydown doesn't make the home cost less. It moves money to the front of the loan to make the first stretch easier.

## Where the money comes from

The upfront cost is significant, and somebody funds it. Sometimes that's a builder or seller incentive, which sounds free until you remember the incentive is a fixed pot. A dollar spent subsidizing year one is a dollar that can't buy your rate down permanently, cover closing costs, or keep cash in your pocket for the furniture and window coverings a new home eats.

And when the buydown is effectively financed through the price of the home, you've increased the debt on the house without adding anything to its long-term value. The payment relief ends. The loan balance doesn't.

> **The trade in one sentence** — A temporary buydown spends real money to rent a lower payment for a while. Whether that's smart depends entirely on what happens when the subsidy runs out.

## When it genuinely fits

The strongest case: your income is very likely to grow. You're finishing a residency, a licensure, a commission ramp, or a scheduled step increase, and the full payment that's tight today will be comfortable in two years. The buydown bridges you into the home now instead of making you wait, and you can point to the specific reason the later payments work.

It can also make sense when a seller is funding it and you've already covered the uses that do more, like a permanent rate reduction, and compared what each one is worth over the years you expect to own.

## When to think twice

The weakest case is the most common one: taking the low payment now and banking on a refinance before the subsidy ends. A refinance only helps if rates materially improve, and nobody can promise you they will. If rates hold or rise, you're simply left with the full payment you qualified at, minus the money that went into the buydown.

If the full payment only works in a version of the future you can't control, the buydown isn't solving the affordability problem. It's postponing it, with interest.

## How to decide

Have both versions run against your actual numbers: the temporary buydown and a permanent rate reduction using the same dollars. Look at the total cost over the years you realistically expect to own, not just the first year's payment. The short-term saving is easy to see. The long-term cost is the part that has to earn its keep.
